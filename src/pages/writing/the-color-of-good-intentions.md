I was sitting with Gino while he colored with a box of crayons I'd bought him last year. They're called *Colors of Kindness*, a set of twenty-four from Crayola, and I remember liking the idea when I bought them. A little encouragement worked into something a child already uses. That seemed lovely.

Then I needed to know what color I was holding.

I'm colorblind. The name on a crayon does work for me that someone else may never have needed it to do. When I can't reliably identify a color by looking at it, I read the wrapper. It's how I know what I'm using.

With these crayons, the encouraging names weren't giving me that information. I could understand the sentiment. I couldn't reliably identify the color. Sitting beside my son, I found myself amused by the contradiction: I'd bought something because I appreciated its kindness, and here was a small part of the experience that made coloring harder for me.

The consequence was almost certainly unintended. That's the part that stayed with me.

## What was the old information doing?

I've spent enough time designing products to recognize how easily this happens. You see an opportunity to improve something familiar. A label could have more personality. An interface could be simpler. An ordinary interaction could carry a little more feeling. The improvement is easy to describe, so it becomes the thing everyone is looking for when they evaluate the work.

What can be harder to see is the job the existing detail was already doing.

If you can identify a crayon's color at a glance, its name may seem like an available place to be expressive. You already have the information. For me, that little piece of text is a way to get information I can't reliably get through sight alone. We can hold the same object and depend on different parts of it.

I don't know how the naming or labeling decisions were made. I haven't audited every wrapper or version of the product, and my experience with this box doesn't tell me what anyone considered during its development. What I do know is that, in the moment I wanted to color with Gino, the names I was reading weren't helping me choose.

That distinction matters. I don't need to invent a careless designer to explain a consequence I experienced. In fact, making someone careless would let the rest of us off a little too easily. This is precisely the kind of thing thoughtful people can miss when the information they are changing has never been necessary to them.

## The improvement depends on where you're standing

There is a reasonable argument for making the names playful. These are children's crayons. Encouraging words can be part of the pleasure of using them, and I liked that enough to buy the box. I still like it.

The question is whether the sentiment and the identifying information need to compete. A recognizable color name alongside an encouraging phrase would give me another way to choose. The precise treatment would need to work on a small wrapper, but the design question is ordinary enough: how do we add meaning while preserving information someone depends on?

I've encountered versions of that question throughout my work. Remove a visible label because an icon seems obvious, and the person who understood the label now has to interpret a picture. Simplify a screen by hiding a control, and someone who could previously find it has to learn where it went. The change may help many people. The person it makes things harder for can still be telling you something true about the design.

Unintended consequences often begin with that unevenness. Something that feels redundant from one person's position is carrying part of the experience for someone else. Unless that dependence becomes visible, removing it can look like an uncomplicated improvement.

That's why intention is such an incomplete way to evaluate a result. It explains what we were trying to accomplish. It can't tell us whether someone can use what we made.

## What happens after we notice?

I don't think the lesson is that we should anticipate everything. We won't. Nor do I want a box of crayons to carry the moral weight of every exclusion anyone has experienced. I was coloring with my son, and a detail on a wrapper made something harder than it needed to be. The scale of the event is small.

But small consequences are useful to notice because we can examine them without pretending the whole effort was a failure. I can appreciate the idea and still describe where it stopped working for me. Both belong in an honest account of the product.

The more demanding question comes after someone points out the problem. Do we treat the original intention as an answer, or do we let the experience change our understanding of what we made? Once a consequence is visible, it becomes something we can respond to. We may still have constraints or competing needs, but we have more information than we had before.

That is a practical kind of empathy. It asks us to take seriously a dependence we may never share, including when doing so complicates an idea we were pleased with.

I still find the moment with Gino funny. A box of crayons offering encouragement, and me trying to work out what color one of them was. I wanted the kindness to work. I also wanted to color with my son without having to guess.

There ought to be room on the wrapper for both.
