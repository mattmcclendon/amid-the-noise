I have been on both sides of a public counter.

I have sat in a welfare office holding a number, waiting inside a system that seemed to regard my presence as evidence of a mistake. The process was difficult to understand. The room communicated compliance more clearly than help. Complexity stayed with the person least equipped to resolve it.

Years later, I worked in election operations for the Santa Clara County Registrar of Voters. People arrived with names that did not match the rolls, addresses from another county, incomplete information, and uncertainty about where they belonged. The system expected this. It allowed participation to continue while moving verification into a process built to reconcile it.

The difference was not the absence of rules.

It was where the system placed the burden of reality.

One pushed complexity onto the person. The other took complexity onto itself.

That distinction has followed me through three decades of work in design and technology. It is also one reason I am considering a more direct role in public service.

## Upstream

Designers learn to find the point where a system and a person stop understanding one another.

Sometimes the repair is visible. A form can be clarified. A workflow can be shortened. A decision can be explained at the moment someone needs to act on it.

Other failures begin farther upstream.

An agency may be operating under a rule that no interface can make humane. A public servant may recognize the right outcome and lack the authority to reach it. A program may be asked to solve a problem while its budget rewards something else. By the time these decisions arrive at the counter, they have hardened into procedure.

I have spent much of my career making consequential systems more legible. That work matters. It does not reach every source of the harm it reveals.

Statutes establish the boundary. Budgets decide what can survive inside it. Oversight determines whether the public promise resembles the public experience.

Those are design decisions too. They are made through public policy.

Policy is shaped through more than one kind of public role. Legislatures establish statutes and budgets. Agencies translate them into programs. State and local commissions examine evidence, hear from the public, and make recommendations or decisions that determine how a policy reaches a community. The language may begin in a hearing room. The consequence arrives somewhere much more ordinary.

At a counter.

In a rent notice.

During a medical appointment.

Inside a decision someone cannot appeal because nobody designed a path back through it.

## The shape of a livable life

Livability can sound like a category of urban policy. I think of it as the accumulated result of many systems either cooperating with a life or making that life harder to hold together.

Housing sits at the base of it.

A stable home affects whether a child remains in the same school, whether an older person stays near familiar care, whether a worker can live near a job, and whether a family has enough money left for everything housing is supposed to make possible. When access to place is distributed almost entirely by wealth, opportunity follows the same map.

Housing equality requires more than producing units. It requires attention to who can reach them, who can remain in them, and which communities are repeatedly asked to absorb displacement as the price of growth.

The same test applies to the systems serving aging Californians. A benefit that exists but cannot be understood is only partially available. Care that requires transportation someone no longer has is not meaningfully accessible. A process built around speed, mobility, digital fluency, or family assistance can quietly exclude the people most dependent upon it.

Public systems often describe equality at the level of rules. People encounter it at the points where rules leave room for discretion.

Who receives patience. Whose documentation is questioned. Which neighborhood carries an environmental burden. Who can afford the delay required to correct an error. Who is believed when safety, discrimination, or bodily autonomy is at stake.

Racial equality, sex equality, and women’s rights become real or unreal at those points. The statute matters. So do enforcement, access, appeal, and the willingness of the state to protect a right when protecting it becomes politically inconvenient.

These issues are separated into departments and committees because institutions require structure. They converge inside a life.

Housing affects health. Caregiving affects income. Transportation affects employment. Discrimination compounds each of them.

Livability is what the system produces when those decisions meet.

## Technology always arrives somewhere

The technology industry often describes infrastructure from the perspective of capability. A community experiences it through land, power, water, roads, employment, noise, tax revenue, and trust.

Data centers make that difference unusually visible.

They support services people use every day and computational systems whose demand is growing quickly. Their necessity does not remove their physical presence. Every facility has an address. Every resource commitment enters a local system already carrying other obligations.

The public conversation rarely gives people enough room to hold both truths.

Residents are often left to choose between corporate assurance and generalized suspicion. One side speaks in technical abstractions. The other fills the resulting uncertainty with fear. By the time a proposal reaches a public meeting, education has been confused with persuasion and trust has already thinned.

I would make data-center education a serious part of my public work.

A constituency should be able to understand what a proposed facility does, which resources it will use, what it will contribute, how its claims will be measured, and what happens if the reality differs from the promise. People should not need an engineering background to evaluate infrastructure that will become part of their community.

Coexistence requires enforceable commitments. It also requires translation honest enough to survive disagreement.

Artificial intelligence brings the same responsibility into less visible territory.

AI systems increasingly influence which information appears, which application receives attention, which case is escalated, and how limited public capacity is directed. The important governance questions begin before a model is described as innovative or dangerous.

What decision is the system shaping? What information does the person receive? Can an error be challenged? Who has the authority to correct it? Which institution remains accountable after the model has produced an answer?

Notice, auditability, recourse, and human responsibility are not restraints on useful technology. They are part of the infrastructure required for people to live with it.

Technology should increase public capacity without reducing the public’s ability to understand or contest what is being done.

## The test is usefulness

There are easier ways to discuss public systems than becoming answerable for one.

Writing can reveal a pattern. Design can reduce harm within an existing structure. Research can give an institution language for something it has been experiencing without understanding.

I intend to keep doing all three.

A formal role in public service adds a different obligation. It connects judgment to a public record, a public process, and a community that has every right to ask what happened after the discussion ended.

That accountability is part of what draws me toward it.

I am not interested in treating exploration as an early performance. The useful questions are harder than whether a message works.

Can my experience help make housing more attainable and public systems more humane? Can I defend equality where it is most vulnerable, in the administrative distance between a right and its enforcement? Can I help California govern emerging technology without asking communities to choose between innovation and control? Can I listen closely enough to distinguish a statewide pattern from a local reality?

A community is not an audience assembled around an institution. It is a place with memory, pressure, disagreement, and knowledge of its own. Public service begins by learning what the system looks like from there.

I know what it is to wait while a public system holds the complexity on my side of the counter.

I also know what it looks like when a public system accepts that complexity as its responsibility.

I can describe the difference.

Public service would make me responsible for helping to change it.
